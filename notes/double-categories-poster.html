Double Categories

Where programs meet their specifications

Programs compose. Specifications compose.
Double categories let them compose together.

Note1 · Two kinds of arrows

An ordinary category has one kind of arrow. A double category has two:

  • tight arrows are programs: things you run;
  • loose arrows are specifications: things you check;
  • squares are evidence that the programs respect the specs.

if \(x \mathrel{\loose{R}} y\) then \(\tight{f}(x) \mathrel{\loose{R'}} \tight{g}(y)\)

Take two different sorting algorithms, and the spec perm, relating two lists that are rearrangements of each other.

insertionSort on [3,1,2] and mergeSort on [2,3,1] both give [1,2,3]. That holds for every pair of permutations, so the square exists. The algorithms agree, however the input may be arranged.

A double category is a pseudo category object in \(\mathbf{Cat}\); loose composition is associative and unital only up to coherent isomorphism [1].

Note2 · You’ve met one: \(\rel\)

Objects are types, tight arrows are functions, loose arrows are relations. A square means the functions preserve the relation.

The relation \(\loose{\mathsf{els}} : \mathsf{List\ Nat} \larr \mathsf{Set\ Nat}\) relates a list to the set of its elements.

If els relates xs to s, it relates cons x xs to insert x s, hence cons implements insert.

Non-example. els relates [1,1] to {1}, but length gives 2 and size gives 1. No square!

A square says this implementation respects that abstraction.

This is data refinement; \(\rel\) is an equipment, where each function also gives a loose arrow, its graph [2].

Note3 · Squares are tiles

Squares glue like floor tiles, in two directions.

Top to bottom, programs compose: run one, then the next.

\((\tight{g} \circ \tight{f})(x) = \tight{g}(\tight{f}(x))\)

Left to right, specs compose: chain relations through a middle point.

\(x \mathrel{(\loose{R} ; \loose{S})} z \iff \exists y.\ x \mathrel{\loose{R}} y \wedge y \mathrel{\loose{S}} z\)

Rows first or columns first gives the same big square (the interchange law): the whole program meets the whole spec.

Squares with identity tight sides form a bicategory of loose arrows; when every function also has a loose companion, the double category is an equipment [2].

Note4 · Searching Trees and Lists

A binary tree can be flattened to a list in many ways. The spec traverse relates a tree to any of its traversals (in-order, pre-order, …), so it is a genuine relation, not a function. Fix a key a: searching the tree and searching the list give the same answer.

Both searches succeed or fail together. Depending on the model of computation, a square can also carry extra data, such as how many comparisons each search makes: it records how the programs agree, not just that they do.

Its traversals, all related to it by traverse:

  • pre-order (root, left, right): [1,2,3,4,5]
  • in-order (left, root, right): [2,1,4,3,5]
  • post-order (left, right, root): [2,4,5,3,1]
  • level-order (top to bottom): [1,2,3,4,5]
Note5 · Where they show up
  • Effects. Types, terms and effectful programs (state, exceptions, I/O) [3,4,5].
  • Gradual typing. Types, terms and type precision \(A \sqsubseteq B\) [6].
  • Open systems. Circuits, Petri nets, reaction networks: maps of interfaces and systems with boundaries [7].
  • Concurrency. Machines, homomorphisms and bisimulations [8].
  • Proof-relevant relations. Sets, functions and proof-relevant relations.
  • Linear logic. Groupoids, functors and distributors [9].
  • Category theory. Categories, functors and profunctors [1,10].
  • Topology. Manifolds, diffeomorphisms and cobordisms.
  • Spans. Sets, functions and spans [1].
  • Quintets. In any 2-category, the 1-cells are both tight and loose, and squares are 2-cells [10].
